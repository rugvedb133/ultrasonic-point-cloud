A 3D scan from one ultrasonic sensor

We put a low-cost HC-SR04 on a two-servo pan/tilt mount, swept it across a scene, and turned every distance reading into a point in space.

  • HC-SR04 sensor
  • MSP430G2 LaunchPad
  • Python

How a distance becomes a point

One reading gives us one point. A scan is many of them, taken at different angles.

  1. The sensor sends an ultrasonic pulse and times the echo. That gives a distance, r.
  2. The microcontroller records r together with the two servo angles at that moment.
  3. It sends those numbers to the PC over a serial link.
  4. A Python script turns each reading into an x, y, z point and plots it.
  5. The servos step to the next angle and the cycle repeats. After several sweeps in both directions, the points add up to a map of the scene.

From angles to x, y, z

The sensor only knows distance. The angles come from the servos. Treat the sensor as sitting at the origin, with tilt φ measured from the vertical and pan θ measured around it. Then:

x = r · sin φ · cos θ
y = r · sin φ · sin θ
z = r · cos φ

This is the standard convention. Where the zero positions sit on the real rig depends on how the servos were mounted.

Sensor at the origin: distance r, tilt φ, pan θ

Drop a few points at different angles and you are building a tiny point cloud. Drag the scene, or focus it and use the arrow keys, to look around.

The mount and wiring

Everything that moves is two micro servos in a pan/tilt kit. Everything that decides is the board.

Pan
The lower servo turns the whole mount through 360°, covering the horizontal plane.
Tilt
The upper servo swings the sensor through 180° vertically.
Control
The board sends PWM pulses, and the pulse width sets the angle.

We calibrated the servos on the Arduino Uno first, so we knew what angle each pulse width gave before moving to the LaunchPad.

What connects to what

  • PowerOne supply feeds the board, the sensor and the servos.
  • ServosThree wires each: signal, power and ground. The signal wire carries the PWM pulses from the board.
  • SensorThe board triggers the HC-SR04 and times its echo to get a distance.
  • PCThe readings and both angles leave the board over a serial link.

Sweeping the scene

The mount does the looking. At each angle, the sensor answers one question: how far away is the nearest thing?

At each position the board takes a reading and logs it along with the two angles. After several sweeps in both directions, the readings add up to a map of the scene.

Why the result is approximate

  • A wide beamThe HC-SR04 does not look at a point. Each reading covers a cone, so fine detail smaller than the beam gets blurred together.
  • A wobbly mountOur report names the mount's stability, along with the sensor's accuracy, as the limit on resolution. Two small servos in a kit can only hold an angle so well.
  • One point at a timeWith a single sensor, a scan takes a while. Several sensors, each covering part of the sphere, would cut the time by a factor of however many we add.

What we got

We scanned a water bottle standing in front of a flat surface.

The shape is recognisable: the bottle comes out as a curved cluster of points, set apart from the flat surface behind it. It is not a smooth surface, though. The points sit in columns with gaps between them, and the edges are soft.

That is what the earlier sections predict. The resolution is bounded by the sensor's accuracy and the mount's stability, so a scan like this shows that one ultrasonic sensor can build a rough 3D map, not a precise one.

What we learned

The scanner works: it turns distances into a recognisable 3D shape. Most of what we learned is about why it is not sharper.

  • The sensor sets the ceiling

    Ultrasound is far lower in frequency than the laser light a LIDAR uses, so it spreads more and every reading blurs over a wide area. We could polish the maths and the plotting all day. The sensor decided how sharp the result could be.

  • We changed boards partway

    We started on an Arduino Uno for early sensor tests and servo calibration, then moved to the MSP430G2 LaunchPad as the main processor. It had the pins, memory and serial link we needed. The price was a second toolchain, Code Composer Studio instead of the Arduino IDE.

  • Mechanics matter as much as electronics

    An angle error gets bigger the farther away the object is: one degree of wobble moves a point at 2 m by about 3.5 cm. We could not fix that in software, and the mount's stability is a limit on resolution.

  • One sensor means a slow scan

    It takes one point at a time. Several sensors, each covering part of the sphere, would cut the scan time by a factor of however many we add.

What we would try next

A higher-frequency sensor would spread less, and several sensors on a custom mount would shorten the scan. These are the obvious next steps on picking up the project again.
